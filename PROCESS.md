# Process — 御风 / Windborne

## Starting idea

This individual project began with the particle bamboo-forest visual language of my earlier martial-arts carnival project. I reduced the scope to one playable encounter: a straw-hatted rider standing on a sword, controlled by small head movements, dodging twelve attacks.

The intended user is someone seated at a laptop. The intended effect is to make a physical gesture visibly change the rider's position and produce understandable feedback. Keyboard mode provides the same loop when the camera is unavailable.

## Decisions and revisions

The Git commits record actual development stages; the history has not been recreated to imply earlier work.

1. **Playable loop first.** Instead of continuing static storyboards, implement four practice gestures, a 60-second trial, scoring, pause/resume and restart. The author chose discrete dodges rather than continuous steering.
2. **Readable action.** The initial scene used pale scenery and linear attack markers. Author feedback described it as monotonous and the timing as unclear. It became a black space with flowing green/gold particles, a particle rider and a giant opponent that visibly winds up and swings.
3. **Camera reliability.** Fix a strict-mode diagnostic callback in the vendored WASM loader. Add camera preview, face bounding box, device selection and restart. Separate actual negative detections from slow inference. Add automatic local compatibility inference when a Worker fails or times out.
4. **Correct direction.** Author testing found right turns moved left and looking up moved down. Reverse the shared yaw/pitch signs. Add tests from camera matrix through calibration offset and gesture gating to rider displacement.
5. **Small downward gestures.** Author feedback found downward movement hard to trigger. Lower its threshold from 9° to 6°, shorten its hold requirement and expose live direction-strength indicators.
6. **Timing and latency.** Feedback reported that users struggled to dodge. Move the actionable warning to one second before impact, display remaining time and extend the trial dodge to 1.4 seconds so early action does not return to centre before impact. Request lower-resolution video, process fresh frames without backlog, raise sampling limits and reduce smoothing delay. Simulated-delay tests do not prove measured real-camera latency.
7. **Keepsake revision.** The original export contained the game scene; its image and download were confirmed by the author. The latest requested version captures the user's camera frame and adds a score-based joke, such as “剑气的已读不回”. It evaluates game performance, not appearance. Images are kept locally, and a missing portrait produces an honest no-photo card.

The earlier promise that camera images were excluded from exports applied only to the original game-scene keepsake; the current home screen and README describe portrait capture explicitly.

## Other-person trial and design response

A classmate who studies kendo tried the game and reported difficulty telling which way to dodge from the opponent's movement. The feedback concerned the direction of the attack itself. A timing cue alone could not communicate it.

I revised the left and right attacks first. The opponent is 12% larger. Both hands lift the sword on the body's centreline, with the forearms and grip aligned along the blade. The blade settles at 45° toward the player's safe side. It turns red one second before impact, holds that posture through the input window, then sweeps diagonally down across to the other side. The sideways practice prompts teach the same rule. The design draws on the diagonal action of 袈裟斩 and the paired directions of 正胴 and 逆胴 as a visual cue for this game.

A further revision raises both hands above the crown into a high guard. A pale blade extends from the hands in 0.22 seconds during the lift. After the red cue, the support hand releases and the sword hand extends through the diagonal cut. The next revision places the robot’s right hand at crown level and its left at chin level. The horizontal draw for vertical dodges takes inspiration from the requested 向之刀其二 movement. The opponent darts to either flank with an enlarged head and a high or low sword plane. A red blade and green block cue the dodge. Each run varies the attack intervals, shortens the later gaps and gives the draw a faster stroke than the diagonal cut. The angled flying sword pivots under the rider’s feet. After visual feedback, the blue sword trail was removed, the rider enlarged and lowered, and the slimmer capsule-shaped legs restored. The opponent now holds a crouched, forward-leaning draw preparation at its starting position. It uses an ordinary head-to-body ratio and rushes to the random flank only after the red warning, swelling in whole-body scale as it approaches. The draw stroke lasts 0.09–0.13 seconds in the game timeline. High and low preparatory hand positions communicate the required dodge before the rush. Exact lineage-specific choreography still awaits a reference performance clip. The participant has not yet evaluated this revision. Whether the controls were explained before their first attempt was not recorded.

## Remaining before final submission

### Follow-up feedback on early height cues and gaze concentration

The author reported that the high and low attacks were still difficult to distinguish. The opponent now moves its entire body upward or downward by one world unit during the opening preparation, before the red cue and rush. This makes the height difference visible earlier while keeping the same high-attack/duck and low-attack/rise mapping.

A second test user suggested centering the timing bar, preferably above the rider, because looking away to the side made it harder to concentrate on the character. I moved the trial timing bar and its short direction/status cue to the horizontal centre above the neutral rider. The green hit window remains visible there. The practice instructions stay in their own panel. These changes respond to the reported observations; a repeat trial of this revision is pending.

## UI revision — 1 November 2026

The author requested an original “90s anime tactical title sequence” UI. I kept the procedural particle forest, characters, attacks and input loop. CSS tokens now separate warm-white neutral controls, purple chapter/selection accents, fluorescent-green recognition/safety feedback and orange-red attacks/errors. Home, pause and results use cropped typography and chapter-card composition; the HUD and local camera preview use numbered telemetry. Visible text and keepsake captions are Traditional Chinese and English. The camera capture, photo selection and download mechanism are unchanged.

Codex used the UI UX Pro Max and Playwright CLI skills for design-system/usability checks and staged browser review. **Kept:** semantic colours, visible keyboard focus, centred timing feedback and reduced-motion support. **Rejected:** the skill's generic marketing layout/palette/type recommendations, in favour of the author's supplied palette, local display fonts and original poster direction. No component library or new visual media was installed.

A development-only query fixture was added before UI changes, with fixed poses and scores for six states. It is excluded from the production bootstrap; the production browser check confirmed the query does not select a results fixture. New fixture regression tests supplement the existing gameplay/camera tests, bringing the total to 52 passing tests. The production build passes.

Playwright review covered all six states at 1440×900, 1280×720 and 390×844. Early screenshots were taken before Three.js had rendered, so later checks explicitly wait for scene readiness. The review also caught a home-screen footnote too close to the bottom at 720px height, mobile practice copy too close to the rider, and a camera-panel placement conflict; these were adjusted. Keyboard practice progressed through all four directions and the visible green cue led to a scored dodge. Pause/resume, focus containment, retry, PNG download, camera-denial feedback, keyboard fallback and reduced-motion preference were exercised. Twelve UI-only screenshot baselines matched on a second pass with WebGL hidden. [Review procedures and evidence](docs/ui-review/README.md) describe the limits.

These are developer/automated checks, not a new human trial. They do not establish live webcam comfort or end-to-end camera latency. The two participants' actual feedback recorded above remains the evidence for the earlier animation and timing changes.


## Faster trial and reversible CRT treatment — 2 November 2026

The author requested 25 attacks, faster slashes based on the earlier late-game feel, and a unified CRT treatment across home, results and the exported portrait. I kept the one-minute trial as a stated assumption: 25 slots average 2.4 seconds, tapering from approximately 2.7 to 2.1 seconds with random jitter. The actionable cue remains one second before impact. Diagonal swing duration is 0.26–0.32 seconds; the horizontal draw is 0.085–0.12 seconds. Gesture thresholds and neutral rearming are unchanged. A 25-direction sequence distributes six or seven attacks per direction and prevents three identical consecutive directions. Scores, rates and both-language roast tiers now share the 25-attack total.

CRT is an optional scoped CSS layer and a matching local PNG drawing style. Large text uses fine raster gaps; small text and controls remain legible. Hatching replaces solid decorative blocks. There is no flashing, rolling scan effect, or added chromatic motion. The home DISPLAY selector and results CLASSIC button restore the prior visual treatment while keeping the 25-attack rules. A Git checkpoint preserves the full earlier version, including the 12-attack game. Portrait capture/selection, the camera pipeline and pause behaviour remain in place; stale exports from another selected style cannot replace the current preview.

The named `landing-page-design` skill was not found in the local skill directories. Codex used the author's concrete art direction, the existing UI UX Pro Max readability guidance and Playwright CLI. No additional UI library, downloaded font or generated image was introduced. Regression tests now include successful completion of all 25 attacks across timing extremes and simulated 0–300ms input delays. These checks do not establish real-camera latency or the comfort of the faster pace. Human feedback on this revision is still pending.

## Public deployment — 2 November 2026

The author requested a playable GitHub Pages link alongside the assignment repository. Codex prepared a GitHub Actions workflow based on Vite's official deployment guide. It installs locked dependencies, runs the existing tests, builds with the `/WingBrone/` asset base, and publishes the generated `dist/` artifact over HTTPS. The existing camera loader already respects Vite's base URL, so gameplay and recognition code did not need modification. Local development retains its original URL. GitHub supplies a short-lived deployment token; no account credential is stored in the project.

The source repository remains the Canvas submission, with the online demo as an extra access route. Existing source, assets, credits, user observations and real commit history are retained. Deployment checks are developer checks, not an additional human user trial, and do not verify real-webcam comfort or latency.
